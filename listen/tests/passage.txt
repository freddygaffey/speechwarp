The lighthouse keeper woke before dawn, as he had done every morning for thirty years. He climbed the narrow stairs, counting each step under his breath, and wound the great clock that turned the lamp. Outside, the sea was calm and grey, and the gulls had not yet begun their noise.

His daughter had written to him that week. She lived in the city now, in a small flat above a bakery, and she said the smell of bread woke her each morning the way the sea had once done. She asked whether he was eating properly, and whether the doctor had been to see his knee. He had not answered yet. Letters were difficult for him; he preferred to say things out loud, to the wind if there was no one else.

At seven the supply boat came round the point. The skipper was a young woman called Morgan, who had taken over from her uncle in the spring. She brought flour, tea, oil for the generator, and a parcel wrapped in brown paper. Inside the parcel was a radio, small and silver, with a note from his daughter that said simply: so you can hear the news, and so you are not alone.

He set the radio on the kitchen table and turned the dial slowly. Music came first, then a man reading the weather for the coastal waters, then a programme about gardening. He listened to all of it. When the gardening programme ended he realised that an hour had passed, and that he had not once looked out of the window at the sea.

That evening he sat down with a pencil and a sheet of paper. He wrote that the radio was a fine thing, that his knee was better, and that Morgan was a good skipper who kept to her times. Then he wrote that he missed her, which he had never written before, and he did not cross it out.

The lamp turned above him through the night, as it always had. Somewhere out on the water a fishing boat saw the light and changed its course, and the crew went safely home.
