The lighthouse keeper had not spoken to anyone for eleven days when the supply boat finally rounded the point. She watched it from the gallery, a small grey shape against a sea that had been restless all week, and she found that she was nervous about having to talk again.

Her work was simple enough to describe and difficult to do well. Every evening she climbed the hundred and twelve steps, trimmed the wick, polished the great lens until it gave back her own tired face, and wound the clockwork that turned the light. Every morning she wrote in the log: the weather, the ships she had seen, the hours the lamp had burned. Nobody read the log. She wrote it anyway, carefully, because one day somebody might.

The boatman tied up at the landing and lifted out crates of oil, flour, tinned fish and a sack of letters. Most of the letters were for the keeper who had lived here before her, a man who had left nine years ago and never told the post office where he had gone. She kept them in a drawer. Some evenings she wondered what they said, and some evenings she nearly opened one, but she never did.

That afternoon the wind dropped, and for the first time in a week the water lay flat and bright. She carried the crates up to the store, made a pot of tea, and sat on the step outside the door with the sun on her hands. Far out, a fishing boat was working the edge of the bank, its gulls wheeling behind it like scraps of paper. She thought about the long winter coming, the storms, the dark that would arrive earlier every day, and found, to her surprise, that she was looking forward to all of it.
